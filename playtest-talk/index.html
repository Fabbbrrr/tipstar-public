How do you test a game nobody has time to play?

ODD PAIR is a two player puzzle game. Two machines were built to play it for us. This is what each one cost, and which one worked.

No game knowledge needed · 10 minutes
Bo carrying the crate. Pip beside him.

Two players, one screen, and neither can do the other's job.

Pip

Small, quick

Squeezes through gaps. Climbs. Reads small print.

Bo

Tall, heavy

Lifts and carries. Reaches high. Holds a thing still.

The socket on the deck wants two plates. Bo can lift the heavy one; only Pip can carry the small one. When one of them needs the other, the game says so on their own screen.

Every room needs both of them, in order.

So testing it means playing it. Again.

A room is a chain of prompts, and any link can break: a prompt that names the wrong object, a step that disappears when you stand on it, a lever that outlives its door. None of them throw an error.

The arithmetic

Four pairs, five acts, seventy five minutes each: twenty five hours of other people's evenings, for one pass each.

The cases we never get to

  • a pad unplugged in the middle of a room
  • a save made halfway through a puzzle
  • a room edited on Thursday, last played on Tuesday

A scripted route proves a room can be finished. It cannot tell you the prompt lied.

So we put a language model in the player's seat.

At Sonnet 5.5 list price, $2 and $10 per million tokens, applied to the measured 57,188 tokens in and 192 out over 40 turns. A projection: Sonnet never played. The harness ran on a local Qwen 3.8 Flash Next, one decode slot, and on a cheap hosted model.

Seventeen cents a run, and not one of them finished the room.

Jev answers a question. It does not write an answer.

One call carries the facts and the questions we want answered about them. What comes back is typed: a pick with the probability of every option, a yes or no as a number, and a score.

Nothing to parse, and nothing to invent.

This is the turn the chat model never made. Pip walks back west to the crate instead of squeezing through the vent, and the run clears from there.

Four options in, one move out, and the room opens.

One turn, five steps, a quarter of a second.

  1. The game hands over what it knows.
  2. Our code builds the menu. Every move allowed now, at most five. Distances become words, never metres.
  3. One call. Which option, is there a move worth taking, whose move the room wants.
  4. Confidence decides. Under 0.35 it takes the information option instead.
  5. The executor walks and presses, through the same input a player uses.

The model cannot invent a move. The menu is the game's own list.

A human playtest

9 seconds, one person, every pass you want.

It cannot talk, and it cannot tell you a room is fun. A clear means the menu was right, not that a player understood the room. The chat harness stays for that.

A fix can be measured before lunch.
