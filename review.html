What the research says (grounding these numbers)

  • Base rate is set by field size. A random pick wins 1/N; our average field was 6.7 dogs, so a random top pick wins ~15%. The market favourite concentrates real win probability - it won 44% here (published AU figures cluster around 30-35%, higher in feature/short fields).
  • The market is very efficient in Australia. The ECU study of AU betting markets ('Late Money and Betting Market Efficiency: Evidence from Australia', 2019) finds the closing market is extremely hard to beat, and that 'late money' keeps repricing right up to the jump. The standard test of a betting system is not hit-rate but Closing Line Value - does it pick prices that end up better than the closing market? A model that cannot beat the closing line is not profitable over time, however often its top pick wins.
  • Bookmakers bake in an overround. AU win markets are priced at roughly 112-130% (tighter at TAB/Sportsbet for feature races, looser at provincial mid-week meetings). We de-vig by dividing each implied probability by that sum - which is why p_mkt is the market's fair estimate, and why even a perfect model has to clear that margin to turn a profit.
  • The favourite-longshot bias is REVERSED in greyhounds. In horse racing, bettors over-bet longshots and under-bet favourites (Snowberg & Wolfers, 2010, show this is driven by misperception, not risk-love, and - critically - it does NOT create a profit opportunity for unbiased bettors). But a peer-reviewed greyhound study (Gulati & Shetty, 2007) found the opposite: bettors over-bet favourites and under-bet longshots, so the market tends to price the favourite slightly too cheap. That is exactly the direction this system's 'edge' tips bet AGAINST (the model underweights the favourite and finds 'value' in mid/long prices) - i.e. the wrong side of the bias in this sport.
  • Handicappers explain little beyond the market. Benter (2008), the classic computer-handicapping study, found the public's market estimate alone already carries a pseudo-R-squared of ~0.12, and a strong fundamental model added only ~0.017 to that - a tiny increment that was nonetheless profitable over ~5 years with fractional Kelly. The point: the market price is the dominant predictor, and a model whose dominant feature is the market price should be expected to sit at, not above, the market - which is precisely what we measured (model Brier 0.713 vs market 0.708).
  • The closest published analogue matches this result. A recent HKJC study built a market-aware logistic model (market price as a feature); it beat the market-free version on log-loss and top-pick win rate, yet betting every top pick still produced negative ROI. The lesson: a marginally better proper score is not profit - the edge must come from selective value, betting only where your probability diverges from a sharp close by more than the takeout.
  • If an edge ever appears, stake with fractional Kelly. Kelly sizing (fraction = edge/price) maximises long-run growth but is very high-variance; practice is to bet 0.25-0.5x Kelly. None of this is relevant until a real, audited edge exists.

What this means for the tips

  • Each tip's 'likelihood' is the model's probability for that dog (the 'Model %' column above). Those numbers are honest relative-value estimates, not certainties: a 76% pick can still lose when it is the model's over-confident outlier.
  • The 114 tips are the high-'edge' contrarians (the model is roughly 3x the market on each). The research and the data agree these are the riskiest bets the system makes - the market is usually right there. 1/114 won here, which is within normal variance but is the realistic picture, not an anomaly.

Bottom line

  • The pipeline works as intended: it trains, scores, and settles reproducibly on real data.
  • On this sample the model is roughly as good as the market, and the market is a little better. It adds little beyond the de-vigged market price, which is the honest expectation for a model whose main feature is the market price.
  • The contrarian 'edge' tips do not reliably pay - a model that over-trusts its disagreement with the market is the classic way to lose.
  • This is one day / ~388 races. It is directional, not a verdict; a real edge claim needs many more settled races and a walk-forward audit (see GAPS.md).
